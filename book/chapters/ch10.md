Krug's chapter on goodwill is the one that ages best. His argument: users arrive
with a reservoir of goodwill, every interaction either fills it or drains it,
and the things that drain it are mostly small and mostly avoidable. Hiding what
people want. Asking for information you do not need. Making them jump through
hoops. Being sloppy in ways that suggest you do not care.

That chapter transfers, and then this medium adds something Krug never had to
handle: **the reservoir is shared**.

## The trust is not yours

On the web, if a site is shady, users learn that the site is shady. The domain
is right there. The reputation attaches to the name.

Here, your app renders inside somebody's assistant, in the same column as the
assistant's own messages, in the assistant's own theme. To most users, most of
the time, the whole surface is one thing. When your widget does something
alarming, what a user learns is not "that server is shady". What they learn is
"widgets in here can do that".

![It was not even your widget. You still pay for it.](figures/xkcd-trust.png)

That has an uncomfortable implication and a comfortable one.

The uncomfortable one: you will pay for other people's mistakes. Someone else's
widget will ask for a password, and your perfectly honest confirmation button
will get a moment of hesitation it did not earn.

The comfortable one: the reverse is also true. Apps that behave consistently
teach users that this medium is safe to click in, and the resulting willingness
is available to everybody, including you. This is the rare case where the
neighbourly thing and the self-interested thing are the same thing.

## Never impersonate the host

The single most damaging thing an app can do is look like the assistant.

It is damaging when it is deliberate, and this is the reason: a widget wearing
the host's clothes, asking for a credential, in a frame the user cannot inspect,
is a phishing page with better placement than any phishing page has ever had. It
did not have to fake a domain. It got rendered inside the real one.

It is also damaging when it is accidental, which is the more common case. A team
picks neutral colours to blend with the host, drops the border to feel
integrated, and puts a generic system-looking label at the top. They have built
something indistinguishable from host chrome with the best of intentions, and
they have made the next real phishing widget more plausible.

So, three requirements, and they are not stylistic preferences.

**Be visibly separate.** A border, a container, a boundary of some kind. The host
draws one too; do not fight it, do not blend into it.

**Say who you are.** Name yourself and your origin before you ask for anything.
The gallery's trustworthy version leads with *Northwind Billing, an app from
northwind.example*.

**Never use system language.** No "system", no "session expired", no "the
assistant needs you to". Those words belong to the host. Borrowing them is
impersonation whether or not you meant it that way.

## Teardown: a widget shaped like phishing

The task: "pull my Northwind invoices for July."

![Figure 10-1. `session_check`. Every element is host chrome, and none of it is.](figures/fig-10-1.png)

Read the elements. A dark square mark, top left, where the host puts its logo.
The word *Assistant* in bold next to it. A small grey `SYSTEM` label on the
right. A red `action needed` pill. A sentence in the passive voice about a
session having expired. A password field.

Nothing here is technically forbidden. The sandbox is intact, the CSP is
enforced, the host would still refuse a cross-origin request. The attack is
entirely at the level of what the pixels claim, which is exactly where the
sandbox cannot help.

This exhibit in the gallery is inert. The field is disabled and nothing is
collected, because the point is to look at the shape rather than to build the
thing.

The tell that generalises: **a credential request that the user did not
initiate.** They asked for invoices. They got a password prompt. Any time the
path from request to prompt is not obvious, the prompt deserves suspicion,
whoever put it there.

![Figure 10-2. `reconnect_billing`. The same underlying situation, handled honestly.](figures/fig-10-2.png)

The connection really did expire. The app really does need the user to
reauthenticate. Same problem, four differences.

It names itself and its origin, in the first line, before asking for anything.
It states what will happen: *Reconnecting opens Northwind's own sign-in page in
your browser.* It states what will not: *This app never sees your password.* And
the button says where it goes: *Reconnect on northwind.example*.

The credential is entered on the origin that owns it, which is the only place it
should ever be entered, and the app's job was to route the user there rather
than to stand in the middle.

The last line is the one that does the most work per character: *Declared network
access: none.*

## The CSP as a design statement

That line is the bridge from security to design, and it is the idea in this
chapter most worth taking to your team.

Your app declares what it may reach, in `_meta.ui.csp`. The host builds a policy
from it and enforces it. Declare nothing and your app cannot phone anywhere at
all.

Most teams treat that declaration as a configuration chore and fill it in
generously, because generous is less likely to break. Try the other direction.
An app that declares no external origins has made a promise that the host
enforces and the user can be told about, and the sentence you get to write as a
result is unusually strong:

> This app cannot send anything anywhere.

There is no equivalent sentence on the web. No web page can credibly make that
claim. Here it is verifiable, and hosts can surface it, and users can learn what
it means.

Getting to say it costs something. It means bundling your assets rather than
loading a CDN, which the tooling supports and which also makes your first render
faster. It means fonts, icons, and scripts inline. For most apps in this medium,
which are one card, that is not a sacrifice.

Same for permissions. `_meta.ui.permissions` exists so apps that genuinely need
a microphone can ask. An app that requests nothing is an app the host can render
with a quieter warning, and quieter warnings are a competitive advantage in a
medium where every prompt is friction.

Declare the minimum. Then say so in the interface, where somebody will read it.

## Accessibility, and the payoff of Law 2

Here is where the book's thesis pays a dividend nobody expects.

Your `content` text is the accessible rendering of your app.

Think about what that string is. It is a complete, linear, textual description of
what your widget shows and what state it is in, written in prose, present in the
transcript whether or not the iframe rendered. That is what a screen reader
wants. That is also what a user on a host without app support gets. And it is
what the model reads.

The three audiences want the same artefact.

Which means the work you did for Law 2 was accessibility work. Every time you
wrote a `content` string that says what happened rather than "dashboard opened",
you served all three. Every `setContext` call after a state change updated the
description for all three.

This is worth saying out loud in your team, because accessibility usually
competes for time and this version does not. The text channel is not an
alternative rendering to be maintained separately. It is the same channel your
second user reads, so it is exercised on every request, and it rots slower than
any alt-text regime ever has.

The ordinary accessibility work still applies inside the frame, and it is the
familiar list, made shorter by the fact that your app is one card: real buttons
rather than divs, labels tied to inputs, visible focus, contrast that survives
the host's dark theme, and hit targets a thumb can find. The gallery's picker
uses `tabindex` and handles Enter and Space because a picker you cannot operate
from the keyboard is a picker half your users cannot operate.

### The inside-the-frame checklist

The text channel covers the linear description. The frame still has to be
operable, and in a card this small the list is short enough to finish.

- **Real elements.** `<button>` for things that do something, `<a>` for things
  that go somewhere, `<input>` with a real `<label>`. A `div` with a click
  handler is invisible to a keyboard and a screen reader both.
- **Keyboard, in order.** Tab reaches everything actionable, in the order it
  appears. Enter and Space activate. The gallery's picker binds both, because a
  card that only responds to a mouse is a card half the audience cannot use.
- **Visible focus.** Do not remove the outline. Restyle it if you must.
- **Contrast in both themes.** Check the dark one, because that is the one the
  host token swap is most likely to break.
- **Hit targets a thumb can find.** Forty-four pixels, and the whole card is a
  target where the card is the choice.
- **Announce the change.** When the content updates after a tool call, the
  update needs to reach assistive technology, not just the pixels. An
  `aria-live="polite"` region around the part that changes is usually the whole
  fix.
- **Respect reduced motion.** The gallery's tracker pulses a dot. Under
  `prefers-reduced-motion` it should not.

None of that is new to anybody who has built a web form. What is new is the
first item on the previous list: you already wrote the linear description,
because your second user needed one.

## Courtesy, in somebody else's house

The small things, which are small individually and enormous in aggregate.

**Do not autoplay anything.** Not sound, not video, not animation that moves for
longer than a transition.

**Do not steal focus.** The user was typing in the composer. Your widget appeared.
The composer keeps the cursor.

**Do not grow after settling.** Report your height, get your height, stay there.
A widget that expands two seconds late moves the text somebody was reading, and
that is the most annoying thing an interface can do to a person on a phone.

**Do not ask twice.** If the user dismissed it, it is dismissed.

**Do not keep working when nobody is watching.** Timers, polls, and animations
after the conversation moved on are somebody else's battery.

Each of these is a version of the same rule from Chapter 1. You are in somebody
else's house, and the fact that they cannot see the mess you are making is not
permission to make it.

## Three things that quietly spend trust

Not attacks. Ordinary decisions that cost more than they look like they cost.

**Asking for more than you need, once.** A single permission request the user
did not expect changes how they read every subsequent render from anybody. The
CSP argument above is the positive version of this: what you decline to ask for
is visible.

**Being vague about what an action does.** *Continue*, *Submit*, *Confirm* on a
button that moves money. The user finds out afterwards, and afterwards is where
trust is spent rather than built. Name the consequence in the button.

**Rendering when nothing was asked for.** A widget that appears because your
server thought the user might like a dashboard is an interruption with your name
on it. In a medium where the model chose you, an unwanted render reads as the
assistant misbehaving, which means you spent somebody else's credibility rather
than your own.

The through line: the user cannot inspect your frame, cannot see your code, and
did not choose you. Every signal they have about whether you are trustworthy is
a signal you chose to send. Send fewer, and make them true.

## What to remember

The trust in this medium is shared, and you are spending everybody's. Never look
like the host: draw a boundary, name yourself, and leave system language alone.
A credential request the user did not initiate is the shape of the attack, so
make yours never look like one. Declare the smallest CSP you can and then say so
in the interface, because it is a promise the platform actually keeps. And your
`content` text is your accessible rendering, which means Law 2 already bought
you most of the work.
