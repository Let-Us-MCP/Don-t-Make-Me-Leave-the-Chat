Anyone who built for the web between about 1998 and 2010 recognises this
situation immediately.

You write one thing. It runs in several environments that agree on the broad
strokes and disagree on the details. Some environments do not support the
feature at all. The list changes quarterly. There is a table on a website
somewhere that is usually a bit out of date.

![One widget, four houses.](figures/xkcd-hosts.png)

The good news, and it is genuinely good: this is a much better version of that
era. MCP Apps is a written specification with a conformance story, hosts declare
their capabilities in a machine-readable way, and the extension negotiates
rather than being sniffed for. Nobody is writing user-agent string parsers.

The rest of the news is that eleven clients render apps today, they were built
by eleven teams, and your widget will look and behave slightly differently in
each of them.

## What actually varies

Appendix B has the generated table. What follows is the shape of the variation,
which matters more than any snapshot of it.

**Whether apps render at all.** The largest difference, and it is binary. A
client either declares `io.modelcontextprotocol/ui` or it does not. Claude Code,
which many readers use every day, does not, because it is a terminal and there
is no iframe to render into. Your app runs there. It just runs as text.

**Viewport.** Widths differ, and so does what happens when your content is
taller than the host wants to give you. Some hosts clip and scroll, some
collapse with a expand affordance, some grant the height.

**Theme.** Every host supplies its own. Some supply a full token set, some
supply light or dark and nothing else. Design so that missing tokens degrade to
something readable rather than to something invisible.

**Which app-initiated calls are allowed.** Hosts may restrict which tools an app
can call, and may disable capabilities like opening links. Treat every
capability as optional and check what came back from `ui/initialize` before
relying on it.

**Consent policy.** Whether a tool call from inside an app prompts, prompts once,
or never prompts is the host's decision and the user's setting. Your design
cannot assume any of them.

**Freshness.** Hosts ship on their own schedule. Your app will be running in a
version of a host that was current eight months ago, in an enterprise that
pinned it.

## Degrade deliberately

Here is the chapter's actual argument.

Every one of those differences produces a degraded experience somewhere. The
question is not whether your app degrades. It is whether the degraded version
is a thing you designed or a thing that happened to you.

![The fallback ladder.](figures/diag-fallback.png)

Five rungs, and you own all five.

**Full app.** The iframe renders, tool calls are allowed, the theme is complete.
Everything works.

**App, no callbacks.** It renders, but the host will not let it call tools.
Anything interactive is decoration now. Does your app know that? It can: check
the capabilities returned from `ui/initialize` and hide or disable the controls
you cannot honour. A button that silently does nothing is worse than no button.

**Static preview.** Some hosts show a thumbnail or a snapshot rather than a live
frame, in a list view or a mobile summary. What does your app look like frozen
at first render, with no data? That is the same question Chapter 3 asked about
skeletons, arriving from a different direction.

**Content text.** No app. The tool ran, the text came back, the model reads it
and answers. **This is not a failure state.** It is the majority state across the
whole ecosystem, and it will stay that way for a while.

**Nothing.** The tool is not called, or the server is not connected. Outside your
control, and the only thing you can do about it is not be the reason.

The rung that decides how good your product feels is the fourth one, and it is
the one that gets the least attention, because it has no pixels to review.

## The text rung is a product

Say it plainly: for a large fraction of your users, your `content` string *is*
your app.

That reframes the work. It is not a fallback you write in ten seconds so the
schema validates. It is the version of your product that runs everywhere, and it
should be as carefully written as the card.

The gallery's probe measures this, because it is measurable:

```
$ node gallery/probe/probe.js http://localhost:8931/mcp
io.modelcontextprotocol/ui  declared
tools         21 (16 carry a ui:// resource)

8/16 apps return a text answer good enough for a host that does not
render apps.
```

Eight out of sixteen. The eight that fail are exactly the book's deliberately
over-built "before" exhibits, which is not a coincidence. The same instinct that
produces a kitchen-sink widget produces a `content` string that says "Loan
analytics suite opened."

Three tests for a text rung that carries its weight:

- **Does it contain the answer?** Not a description of where the answer is.
- **Could the model act on it three turns later?** Names, numbers, identifiers.
- **Does it say what the human can do next?** "The user can change the horizon
  in the app" tells the model what to offer if the app never rendered.

## Testing across hosts without owning eleven of them

You will not install all of them. Nobody does. Here is the practical ladder.

**Test the two extremes.** One host that renders apps and one that does not.
Between them they cover the failure modes that matter: the render, and the text.
Claude and Claude Code are a workable pair and most readers already have both.

**Test with capabilities removed.** Your own mini host can lie about what it
supports. The gallery's returns a capabilities object from `ui/initialize`;
change it, reload, and watch your app find out that it cannot call tools. Doing
this once will find a bug.

**Test the narrow viewport.** 380 pixels, and then 320, because somebody is on a
small phone with a large font size.

**Test both themes,** and test switching while the app is on screen.

**Do not write a compatibility table by hand.** It rots silently, which is worse
than being absent, because people trust it. Appendix B is generated from a JSON
file that carries its source and the date it was checked, so a reader can see
how stale it is rather than guess.

## The part that will change

Some honesty about the shelf life of this chapter.

The current situation is early. Capabilities will converge, hosts will support
more, and some of the differences described here will be gone in a year. The
matrix in Appendix B will be wrong before this book is old, which is exactly why
it is generated and dated.

What will not change is the structure. There will always be a host that does not
render, a version that is behind, and a user whose settings are stricter than
you expected. The fallback ladder is not a workaround for an immature
ecosystem. It is the permanent shape of building for somebody else's client, and
the web spent twenty years learning that the graceful version is cheaper than
the alternative.

## What to remember

Eleven clients render apps and they differ in viewport, theme, capabilities, and
consent policy. Your app degrades, and your only choice is whether the degraded
version is designed. Five rungs, and the fourth one, plain text, is the one most
of your users get. Test two hosts rather than eleven, lie to your own app about
capabilities to see what breaks, and generate your compatibility table so its
staleness is visible.
