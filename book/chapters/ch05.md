::: {.law data-n="4"}
Omit needless widgets.
:::

Strunk and White told writers to omit needless words. Krug pointed out that
about half the words on any web page could go without anyone noticing, and that
removing them made the remaining half easier to find. The same arithmetic
applies here at a much higher exchange rate, because your viewport is a fifth
the size and your user's attention is a tenth as long.

![The curve turns over sooner than the roadmap assumes.](figures/xkcd-omit.png)

The exchange rate is worth stating precisely, because it changes what you cut. A
needless word costs a reader about a fifth of a second. A needless *widget*
costs a fetch, a viewport, a consent surface, a decision about whether to
engage, and the space where the answer would have been. Everything is more
expensive here, so the threshold moves a long way down.

But needless does not mean absent, and three distinctions keep this law from
turning into minimalism theatre. There is a difference between a needless widget
and needless information: the fixed picker later in this chapter drops seven
columns from the pixels and keeps every one of them in `structuredContent`, so
the user sees less while the system knows exactly as much. There is a difference
between a needless control and a needless capability: the horizon slider in
Chapter 1 survived because the user acts on it, the export button did not, and
exporting is still perfectly possible because the user can ask and the model can
call a tool. And there is a difference between needless chrome and needless
identity: Chapter 10 requires you to say who you are, which is one line and
load-bearing, and which is not the same as a logo, a nav bar, and a notification
bell with a red dot on it.

## The porting instinct

The most common way to get this wrong is not to over-design. It is to port.

Somebody has a product. The product has a dashboard. MCP Apps arrive, the
dashboard is already built, and putting it in an iframe takes an afternoon, so
it ships. What arrives in the conversation is a 1200 pixel application squeezed
into a 380 pixel column, with a nav bar for pages the user cannot reach, a
search box for content the conversation already narrowed, and a header
containing a logo, a settings gear, and that notification bell.

Every one of those elements was correct in the product. The nav bar is correct
when there are pages. The search box is correct when the user arrived with no
context and no assistant. The bell is correct when this is a place where
somebody lives. None of them is correct in a paragraph, and the reason porting
fails is that it moves the *artefact* instead of the *job*. The job your product
does for this user, in this conversation, in the next thirty seconds, is a small
fraction of your product, and it is a fraction with different edges than any
screen you have already built.

## The six archetypes

Nearly every useful MCP App is one of six things. Knowing which one you are
building tells you what to keep and what to cut, and more usefully it tells you
what you owe each of your two users, which is a question most teams never ask
explicitly.

The **picker** exists because the human is choosing one thing from a small set.
It owes the human a small set, differentiated at a glance, committed in one tap.
It owes the model the choice, written back, plus enough about it to act on. It
fails when the set is not small: a picker over 400 rows is a search interface
wearing a picker's clothes, and search interfaces belong to the model rather
than to your widget, so let the model narrow and then pick.

The **form** exists because the human has information the system does not. It
owes the human the fewest fields that are genuinely unknown, with prefilled
context shown rather than asked and correction one click away. It owes the model
the same constraints in the schema that the UI enforces, plus the submitted
result written back. It fails when fields outnumber unknowns, which is Chapter 4
and also the humbling case that opens Chapter 9.

The **dashboard** exists because the human is checking on something. It owes the
human a verdict, a reason, and everything else behind a disclosure, and it owes
the model that verdict as a sentence, because "how are things" will be asked
again in text. It fails when there is no verdict: a dashboard that only presents
numbers has delegated the synthesis back to the person who asked you to do it.

The **viewer** exists because the human needs to see a thing, a document or an
image or a model or a chart. It owes the human the relevant part, at a useful
size, with the reason it is relevant marked on it. It owes the model a text
rendering of what is on screen, because the model cannot see pixels and the user
will ask about them. It fails when it rebuilds the browser, and zoom, rotate,
print, download, page navigation, and a thumbnail strip together constitute
rebuilding the browser.

The **tracker** exists because the human is watching something change. It owes
the human the current state, the time that state was read, and a way to refresh
it. It owes the model the same, so that a question about it does not require a
new render. It fails when it stops looking and keeps asserting, which is Chapter
7.

The **canvas** exists because the human is manipulating something directly,
since describing it in words would be slower. It owes the human direct
manipulation that feels immediate, and it owes the model the result of that
manipulation, in structured form, at every resting point. It fails when it is
opaque, which is Figure 2-1.

### The archetype card

One table, for taping next to the laws.

| Archetype | The human's job | The one thing to get right | Owes the model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Picker | Choose one of a few | Differentiated at a glance, one tap | The choice, and enough about it to act |
| Form | Supply what is missing | Only the genuinely unknown fields | Same constraints in the schema |
| Dashboard | Check on something | A verdict, not a pile of numbers | The verdict as a sentence |
| Viewer | See a specific thing | The relevant part, marked | A text rendering of what is shown |
| Tracker | Watch something change | The time it was read | State plus timestamp |
| Canvas | Manipulate directly | Immediacy | The result at every resting point |

The rightmost column is the one people discover late. Every archetype owes the
model something different, and in every case it is precisely the thing the
pixels are carrying that the API is not.

## Knowing which one you are

Most confused widgets are two archetypes fighting. A dashboard with a form in it
means the user came to check on something and got asked a question. A viewer
with a picker in it means the user came to read one clause and got a table of
contents. A canvas with a dashboard around it means the user came to move things
and got metrics about the things they have not moved yet.

When you cannot tell which archetype you are building, that is the finding
rather than a gap in the framework. Split it. Two renders, each with one job,
beat one render with two, because the conversation is a perfectly good container
for a sequence and your widget is a poor one.

There is a seventh thing people build, and it is not on the list because it is
not an archetype. It is a habit. The **launcher** is a widget whose job is to be
a menu of your other widgets: four buttons reading Reports, Settings, History,
Help. It exists because somebody could not decide which of the six the task
needed, so they shipped a hallway. Launchers fail hard here for a reason
specific to the medium: the model already is the launcher. It has your tool
list, it has your descriptions, and it routes based on what the user said, which
is a considerably better routing signal than four buttons. A launcher widget is
a worse version of something the host already does, occupying the space where
the answer would have been. The tell that you are building one is that the
render answers no question. It offers. If a user could look at it and say "and?",
it is a hallway.

## Composing archetypes across renders

Splitting does not mean shipping less. It means shipping the parts in sequence,
which the transcript is unusually good at holding.

A travel booking is a picker, then a form, then a tracker. Built as one widget
it is a wizard with three internal states, a back button you had to write, and a
model that sees one tool call and one result and has no idea which state anybody
is in. Built as three renders it is three tools, three descriptions, three
schemas, and a conversation that reads like a record of what happened. The
second version has a property the first cannot have: the user can leave in the
middle and come back by asking, because "actually, go back to the flights" is a
tool call with arguments rather than a state inside a widget nobody can address.

The cost is real, so this is not a free rule. Three renders is three consent
moments and more scrolling, and decomposing a single decision into three cards
for the sake of purity makes things worse. Decompose when the steps are
separately meaningful and keep them together when they are not; Chapter 8 has
the full decision procedure.

## When the right number is zero

This deserves to be said plainly, because it is the option nobody defends in a
planning meeting. Sometimes the correct MCP App is no MCP App.

A verdict is a sentence: "yes, you can cancel with 90 days notice"
communicates nothing visual and needs no card. Under about five rows and three
columns, a Markdown table in `content` is fine, and it streams, copies, reads
aloud, costs nothing, and can be quoted by the model later. A structured
question is an elicitation, rendered by the host in the host's own style, in
every host, including the ones that do not do apps at all. A confirmation is a
sentence and a tool call: "I'm about to void EXP-40122, confirm?" needs no
chrome whatsoever.

Every one of those options is faster than your widget, works in more places than
your widget, and survives being scrolled past in a way your widget does not.
Shipping zero widgets for a given tool is not a failure to adopt the platform.
It is the platform working as designed: MCP Apps are an option, they negotiate
as an extension, and a server that renders exactly where rendering helps is a
well-designed server rather than a lazy one.

## Teardown: one over-built picker

The task: "get me to New York Thursday morning, economy, just me."

![Figure 5-1. `search_flights`. A web page wearing a chat costume.](figures/fig-5-1.png)

Six filters, all six re-asking things the sentence contained. Ten columns:
flight, departure, arrival, stops, duration, aircraft, cabin, fare, bags, and
CO2. Pagination, for three results. In a 380 pixel column the table scrolls
horizontally, which means the fare, the one column everybody looks at first, is
off screen when the widget first paints. The aircraft type is interesting to
about one traveller in fifty, and it is on screen at the same visual weight as
the price, on every render, for everybody.

![Figure 5-2. `pick_flight`. Three options, one tap, filters resolved into a sentence.](figures/fig-5-2.png)

Three cards. Departure and arrival as the headline, because that is how people
actually choose flights, with the fare as the second thing and duration and
flight number underneath. Two tags, `cheapest` and `fastest`, because those are
the two comparisons that drive most decisions and computing them costs nothing.
The six filters became one line of grey text, *SFO to JFK, one passenger,
economy*, so everything the filters would have set is stated where the user can
check it and nothing is being asked.

The other seven columns are gone from the render and not from the system. They
are in `structuredContent`, so the model has them, and if the user asks "which
one is the widebody" the answer comes back in text without another render. That
is the decomposition this chapter is about: one widget became a smaller widget
plus a sentence, and the information that left the pixels went somewhere it can
still be used.

## The decomposition test

When a widget feels overloaded, run this in order. What is the one question this
render answers? Write it down, and if you write down two, you have two widgets.
What is on screen that does not serve that question, and can it move to
`structuredContent`, to a disclosure, or to the bin? What is on screen that a
sentence would serve better, and can it move to `content`? And finally, what
remains, and does it still beat prose?

Step four is the one people skip, and it is the one that occasionally tells you
to delete the widget entirely. That is a good outcome and a cheap one, and it is
considerably cheaper to learn now than after the launch.

## What to remember

Six archetypes cover nearly everything: picker, form, dashboard, viewer,
tracker, canvas. Each owes the human one thing and the model another, and
confusion about which one you are building shows up as an overloaded widget
whose fix is to split rather than to compress. The seventh thing, the launcher,
is a hallway and the model is already better at it than you are. And the right
widget count is sometimes zero, which is a decision you are allowed to make on
purpose.
