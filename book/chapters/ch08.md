Krug's chapters on navigation are about a simple, unglamorous need: users have
to know where they are, what else is here, and how to get back. The web solved
that with an enormous amount of shared infrastructure. The URL names the place,
the back button undoes the move, tabs hold parallel places, bookmarks make a
place durable, and breadcrumbs show the hierarchy. Every user knows all of it,
because every site has it, which is why site-specific navigation schemes have
always lost to browser affordances.

You get none of it.

![The affordances you do not get, and the ones that replace them.](figures/xkcd-navigation.png)

## What replaces each thing

The URL is replaced by the sentence. The user does not navigate to your app;
they say something and the model picks it, so the addressing scheme for your
application is natural language resolved against your tool descriptions and
schemas. That has a direct implication most teams never act on: your tool
description and input schema *are* your URL structure. They are how a user
reaches a specific state of your app. A tool with an empty schema has exactly
one address. A tool with `horizonYears`, `origin`, `destination`, and `date` has
as many addresses as those values allow, and the user can reach any of them by
saying so. Naming matters for the same reason it mattered in URLs, and more:
`compare_rates` is guessable while `loan_analytics_suite_v3` is not, and the
model's guess is the only thing standing between the user and your app.

The back button is replaced by saying so. "No, go back to the five-year view"
works, and works well, but only under the condition from Chapter 7: the tool
must accept the parameter, and the model must know the value. Do not build your
own back button. A back arrow inside a widget is one of the most reliable ways
to confuse people in this medium, because it looks like the browser's back
button and does something else entirely. If it undoes a step in your flow, label
it with the step, so `Back to dates` rather than a bare arrow.

Tabs are replaced by separate renders, because two things the user might want to
look at are two messages rather than two tabs. The transcript is a perfectly
good container for parallel things and it has one enormous advantage over tabs:
both are visible at once if you scroll, and both are addressable by asking. Tabs
inside a 380 pixel widget are almost always a sign that two archetypes are
fighting, and Figure 1-2 has four of them.

Bookmarks are replaced by the transcript itself, which is durable, searchable,
and shareable, and in which a render that happened is still there. That is a
better bookmark than a bookmark, with one condition: the render has to be
re-summonable, which is the Chapter 7 contract again. And breadcrumbs are
replaced by one line of text at the top of the widget. *SFO to JFK, one
passenger, economy.* *Clause 7.3, page 41 of 84.* *Comparing over 5 years.* That
line does the work of a breadcrumb trail, a page title, and an orientation cue,
and it costs a single row.

## Orientation, in one line

That last replacement deserves its own section, because it is the cheapest
navigation feature in the medium and it is routinely skipped. One line of text
saying where you are does the work of three web affordances, and it also
survives the screenshot somebody takes and pastes into Slack, which no amount of
interactive state does.

Three rules for that line. State the scope rather than the tool, so *SFO to JFK,
one passenger, economy* rather than *Flight Search Results*, because the user
knows they searched and wants to know what was searched for. Include what was
assumed, which is where Chapter 4's origin line and this chapter's breadcrumb
turn out to be the same sentence doing two jobs: anything you filled in on the
user's behalf belongs in it. And keep it to one line, because if it needs two
you are describing too much state, which usually means the widget is holding too
much.

## Multi-step flows

The hard case. The user has to do three things in order. Where do the steps go?
There are three shapes and each breaks in a different place.

**The wizard inside one widget** is step one, next, step two, next, step three,
done: one render, internal state. It works when the steps are genuinely
sequential, short, and meaningless separately, like choosing a seat and then
choosing a meal. It breaks when the user wants to go back and the back is yours
to implement, and it breaks worse when the conversation moves on mid-wizard and
the widget becomes a half-finished form nobody will return to. It breaks worst
for the second user, which sees one tool call and one result and has no idea the
human is on step two of three unless you say so on every step. If you build a
wizard, write back at every step rather than at the end, because the end may not
arrive.

**Multiple renders, one per step** means the tool is called three times and
three widgets appear in the transcript in order. It works when each step is
meaningful on its own and the model has something to do between them: pick a
flight, then confirm the fare, then choose a seat reads naturally as a
conversation, because it is one. It breaks when the steps are trivial, since
three renders for one logical action is three consent prompts and a lot of
scrolling and nobody wants a widget that says "next" three times. It also breaks
when the earlier renders go stale, which they will, because they are still on
screen; the gallery's picker replaces itself with a confirmation on selection
for exactly that reason.

**One card with progressive disclosure** puts everything on one card with the
secondary parts collapsed, so nothing is sequential and the user opens what they
care about. It works when the steps are not really steps, and most "three-step
flows" turn out to be one decision plus two defaults somebody was afraid to
assume. It breaks when there genuinely is an order and a required step is hidden
inside a disclosure the user never opened.

The test that chooses between them is whether the user can do the steps in any
order. If yes, one card with progressive disclosure. If no, and each step
matters on its own, multiple renders. If no, and the steps are meaningless
separately, one widget with a write-back at every step. The gallery's expense
confirmation is the first shape: what looked like a nine field form is one
decision, four assumptions shown as facts, and a disclosure for corrections. It
was never a flow. It was a form pretending to be one.

## Teardown: getting to a clause

The task: "what does the contract say about getting out of it early?"

![Figure 8-1. `open_document`. A browser, rebuilt inside a paragraph.](figures/fig-8-1.png)

Ten controls: page back, page forward, a page number field, zoom out, zoom
level, zoom in, rotate, search, print, download. A thumbnail strip. And page 1
of 84. Every one of those controls exists because a document viewer on the
desktop needs it, and in a chat they are answers to questions the user did not
ask. The user asked about termination, which is on page 41. The navigation model
here is that the user reads the toolbar, finds search, types the word they
already said out loud, reads the result list, clicks one, and arrives: five
interactions to reach a place the model could have gone to directly.

There is a second failure worth naming. The model cannot see the pixels, so
after this renders, if the user asks "so what is the notice period?", the model
has `pageText` for page 1 and nothing else. It will answer from the wrong page
or not at all, and either way the user experiences an assistant that opened a
document and then stopped being useful.

![Figure 8-2. `find_clause`. Navigation resolved before the render.](figures/fig-8-2.png)

The topic became an argument: `find_clause({ topic: "early termination" })`. The
server did the finding, so the render arrives already at the answer, with the
governing words marked and the location stated as a breadcrumb reading *page
41*. Two controls remain and both earn it: previous and next match, because
there are three clauses and comparing them is the actual task, and one escape
hatch labelled *Open full document* for the case where the user really does want
all 84 pages. Every match is written back with `setContext`, so the follow-up
question is answerable in text without another render, and the user can also
navigate by talking: "what about the one on page 58?"

The lesson generalises well past documents. Navigation is usually a search
problem, and search is the model's job. Every step of navigation you put in your
widget is a step you took away from the interface that is better at it.

## The widget that has already been used

A problem with no web analogue, and it catches everybody once. Your picker
rendered. The user picked. The conversation moved on. Your picker is still on
screen, five messages up, looking exactly like it did before anybody touched it.

Now the user scrolls back. In the worst version they see three selectable
options, one of which they already chose, with no indication of which, and
clicking one again either duplicates the action or fails confusingly. Three ways
out, in increasing order of effort. Replace yourself, swapping the widget's
content on commit for a confirmation of what was chosen, when, and what the user
can say to change it; the gallery's picker does this in four lines and it is
almost always the right answer. Show the state, keeping the options visible but
marking the chosen one and disabling the rest, which is right when the set
itself is the useful record. Or re-fetch when it scrolls into view, which is
rarely worth it and spends a tool call on a widget nobody is acting on.

The general rule is that a render is a record of a moment and should look like
one. A widget still offering a decision the user already made is asserting that
nothing has happened, which is the Chapter 7 problem wearing different clothes.

## The escape hatch

One navigation control consistently earns its place: the way out. Some tasks
genuinely exceed what a card in a conversation can do, like reading all 84
pages, editing the pivot table, or approving something that legally needs a
signed session. For those, the honest move is to send the user somewhere else,
and the honest way to do it is to say so.

Label it with the destination, so *Open full document*, *Reconnect on
northwind.example*, or *Continue in the console*, and never *Open* and never an
icon alone. Make it one control at the edge of the card, because an escape hatch
competing with the primary action for attention will win sometimes, and every
one of those times is a user who left when they did not need to. And write the
state back before they go, so the model knows the user left and why, and "did
that work?" three minutes later is answerable; the gallery's reconnect card
writes *sent the user to Northwind's own sign-in page* before it opens anything.

An escape hatch is not a failure of the medium. Refusing to build one, and
instead reconstructing the console inside a 380 pixel column, is.

## Deep linking, by asking

One consequence deserves stating on its own, because it is a genuinely nice
property of this medium rather than a consolation prize.

If your tool's arguments describe the interesting states of your app, then every
one of those states is reachable by a sentence, from any point in any
conversation, without a link, a route table, or a router. "Show me the clause
about confidentiality." "Compare those at three years." "Pull up the deploy for
2026.8.12." That is deep linking, and you got it for the price of writing a
decent input schema. It is the compensation for losing the URL, and it is a
better deal than it sounds, as long as you hold up your end of the contract in
Chapter 7.

## What to remember

No URLs, no back button, no tabs, no bookmarks. The sentence is the address, the
schema is the address space, the transcript is the history, and one line of text
is the breadcrumb. Multi-step flows have three shapes and the choice is decided
by whether order matters. A render that has already been used should say so.
And navigation inside your widget is usually search done badly, when search
belongs to the model.
