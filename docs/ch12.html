Chapter 12

Hosts Are the New Browsers

The compatibility reality, and a fallback ladder you design rather than suffer.

Anyone who built for the web between about 1998 and 2010 recognises this situation immediately.

You write one thing. It runs in several environments that agree on the broad strokes and disagree on the details. Some environments do not support the feature at all. The list changes quarterly. There is a table on a website somewhere that is usually a bit out of date.

The good news, and it is genuinely good: this is a much better version of that era. MCP Apps is a written specification with a conformance story, hosts declare their capabilities in a machine-readable way, and the extension negotiates rather than being sniffed for. Nobody is writing user-agent string parsers.

The rest of the news is that eleven clients render apps today, they were built by eleven teams, and your widget will look and behave slightly differently in each of them.

What actually varies

Appendix B has the generated table. What follows is the shape of the variation, which matters more than any snapshot of it.

Whether apps render at all. The largest difference, and it is binary. A client either declares io.modelcontextprotocol/ui or it does not. Claude Code, which many readers use every day, does not, because it is a terminal and there is no iframe to render into. Your app runs there. It just runs as text.

Viewport. Widths differ, and so does what happens when your content is taller than the host wants to give you. Some hosts clip and scroll, some collapse with a expand affordance, some grant the height.

Theme. Every host supplies its own. Some supply a full token set, some supply light or dark and nothing else. Design so that missing tokens degrade to something readable rather than to something invisible.

Which app-initiated calls are allowed. Hosts may restrict which tools an app can call, and may disable capabilities like opening links. Treat every capability as optional and check what came back from ui/initialize before relying on it.

Consent policy. Whether a tool call from inside an app prompts, prompts once, or never prompts is the host’s decision and the user’s setting. Your design cannot assume any of them.

Freshness. Hosts ship on their own schedule. Your app will be running in a version of a host that was current eight months ago, in an enterprise that pinned it.

Degrade deliberately

Here is the chapter’s actual argument.

Every one of those differences produces a degraded experience somewhere. The question is not whether your app degrades. It is whether the degraded version is a thing you designed or a thing that happened to you.

Five rungs, and you own all five.

Full app. The iframe renders, tool calls are allowed, the theme is complete. Everything works.

App, no callbacks. It renders, but the host will not let it call tools. Anything interactive is decoration now. Does your app know that? It can: check the capabilities returned from ui/initialize and hide or disable the controls you cannot honour. A button that silently does nothing is worse than no button.

Static preview. Some hosts show a thumbnail or a snapshot rather than a live frame, in a list view or a mobile summary. What does your app look like frozen at first render, with no data? That is the same question Chapter 3 asked about skeletons, arriving from a different direction.

Content text. No app. The tool ran, the text came back, the model reads it and answers. This is not a failure state. It is the majority state across the whole ecosystem, and it will stay that way for a while.

Nothing. The tool is not called, or the server is not connected. Outside your control, and the only thing you can do about it is not be the reason.

The rung that decides how good your product feels is the fourth one, and it is the one that gets the least attention, because it has no pixels to review.

Checking capabilities, in three lines

The rung you land on is knowable at runtime, and almost nobody checks.

app.connect().then(function (host) {
  var can = host.capabilities || {};
  if (!can.toolCalls) {
    // Nothing this app can do will reach the server. Say so, once, quietly,
    // and stop rendering controls that would lie about it.
    document.body.classList.add("read-only");
  }
  if (!can.openLink) escapeHatch.remove();
});

Three consequences worth designing rather than discovering:

  • No toolCalls. Your app is a viewer now. Hide the actions, keep the information, and make sure the content text carried the answer.
  • No openLink. Your escape hatches are inert. Remove them rather than leaving a button that does nothing.
  • A partial theme. Tokens you expected are missing. Every colour you use needs a fallback in your own stylesheet, so a host that supplies two variables instead of twelve produces a plain widget rather than an invisible one.

The pattern generalises: treat every capability as absent until the handshake says otherwise. It costs one branch and it removes a whole category of “works on my host”.

The other SDK in the room

ChatGPT shipped an Apps SDK before this extension was final, and it informed the design. The result is that ChatGPT renders MCP Apps and also has its own predecessor with its own conventions.

If you are targeting both, the differences you will meet are in the names rather than the ideas: how the UI resource is attached, what the app-to-host methods are called, and which host capabilities exist. Everything this book argues about survives the translation, because none of it is about the method names. The laws are about what to render and what to write back, and both SDKs give you somewhere to put both.

Check the current shape in the ext-apps specification and in OpenAI’s own docs before you write an adapter. Any table printed here would be wrong by the time you read it, which is the same reason Appendix B is generated.

The text rung is a product

Say it plainly: for a large fraction of your users, your content string is your app.

That reframes the work. It is not a fallback you write in ten seconds so the schema validates. It is the version of your product that runs everywhere, and it should be as carefully written as the card.

The gallery’s probe measures this, because it is measurable:

$ node gallery/probe/probe.js http://localhost:8931/mcp
io.modelcontextprotocol/ui  declared
tools         21 (16 carry a ui:// resource)

8/16 apps return a text answer good enough for a host that does not
render apps.

Eight out of sixteen. The eight that fail are exactly the book’s deliberately over-built “before” exhibits, which is not a coincidence. The same instinct that produces a kitchen-sink widget produces a content string that says “Loan analytics suite opened.”

Three tests for a text rung that carries its weight:

  • Does it contain the answer? Not a description of where the answer is.
  • Could the model act on it three turns later? Names, numbers, identifiers.
  • Does it say what the human can do next? “The user can change the horizon in the app” tells the model what to offer if the app never rendered.

Testing across hosts without owning eleven of them

You will not install all of them. Nobody does. Here is the practical ladder.

Test the two extremes. One host that renders apps and one that does not. Between them they cover the failure modes that matter: the render, and the text. Claude and Claude Code are a workable pair and most readers already have both.

Test with capabilities removed. Your own mini host can lie about what it supports. The gallery’s returns a capabilities object from ui/initialize; change it, reload, and watch your app find out that it cannot call tools. Doing this once will find a bug.

Test the narrow viewport. 380 pixels, and then 320, because somebody is on a small phone with a large font size.

Test both themes, and test switching while the app is on screen.

Do not write a compatibility table by hand. It rots silently, which is worse than being absent, because people trust it. Appendix B is generated from a JSON file that carries its source and the date it was checked, so a reader can see how stale it is rather than guess.

Version skew, which is the quiet one

Compatibility differences you can see are the easy half. The dangerous half is the same host, an older build.

Somebody is running a client from eight months ago because their enterprise pinned it. Their host speaks an earlier protocol revision, supports fewer capabilities, and knows nothing about the field you added last month. Your server is current. Nothing errors. Things just quietly do less.

Three habits, and the first one is free.

Never require a field you added recently. Additions are safe; new required fields are a breaking change, whatever the version number says.

Handle an older negotiated revision by answering in it. The gallery accepts whatever version the client offers and answers with one that client can speak. This is nine lines and it is the difference between a server that works for everybody and a server that is correct and unusable.

Assume unknown capabilities are absent. Covered above, and it is the same rule pointing at the future rather than the present.

Working out that this mattered took one command. claude mcp list reported tools fetch failed, because a client negotiating 2026-07-28 requires resultType on list results and the server was not sending it. Three fixes later it connected. None of the three had anything to do with MCP Apps, and all three are in gallery/VERIFICATION.md, because a reader building on this book will hit exactly the same wall on their first afternoon.

The part that will change

Some honesty about the shelf life of this chapter.

The current situation is early. Capabilities will converge, hosts will support more, and some of the differences described here will be gone in a year. The matrix in Appendix B will be wrong before this book is old, which is exactly why it is generated and dated.

What will not change is the structure. There will always be a host that does not render, a version that is behind, and a user whose settings are stricter than you expected. The fallback ladder is not a workaround for an immature ecosystem. It is the permanent shape of building for somebody else’s client, and the web spent twenty years learning that the graceful version is cheaper than the alternative.

A worked degradation

One widget, four houses, same tool call. This is the gallery’s service status card, and it is the shortest way to see that the ladder is a design rather than a fallback.

In a host that renders apps. The card. Pill, headline, sentence, three numbers, a disclosure holding the per-service table. Half a second to the answer.

In a host that renders but blocks tool calls. Identical, because this widget has no actions. That is not luck; a dashboard should not have actions, and this one earning nothing from toolCalls is the archetype behaving correctly. A tracker in the same position loses its refresh button and has to say so.

In a host showing a static preview. The first render, frozen, with no data. Which is why the skeleton draws the title and the shape rather than a spinner.

In Claude Code, which is a terminal. The text:

degraded: Checkout is slow, nothing is down. One dependency is timing out
on 4% of calls. Everything else is nominal. error rate 4.1%, p99 latency
2.4s, requests/min 18.2k, as of 09:41 UTC.

Every level of the Chapter 3 hierarchy is in there, in order: verdict, reason, numbers, timestamp. A user in a terminal gets the same answer in the same sequence, and the model can act on it without any of the pixels.

That is what a designed fourth rung looks like. The before version of this widget returns “Ops overview opened.”, which is the same tool, the same data, and nothing at all for four fifths of the ecosystem.

What to do about the host you cannot test

Somebody will report that your widget is broken in a client you have never opened. Here is the triage that works without installing it.

Ask which rung. Did the widget render at all? If no, it is a capability question and the answer is your text rung. If yes but nothing works, it is toolCalls. If yes but it looks wrong, it is theme or viewport.

Ask for the text. Whatever the host showed instead, or alongside. That tells you what your content string looks like in the wild, which is worth knowing regardless of the bug.

Reproduce by subtraction, not addition. Take your own mini host and remove the capability you suspect. This finds the bug faster than installing the client would, and it leaves you with a regression test.

Fix at the rung, not at the host. A conditional keyed on host name is a user-agent string with extra steps, and the web spent a decade proving where that leads. Fix it by handling the capability being absent, which fixes it for every other client that also lacks it, including the ones that do not exist yet.

What to remember

Eleven clients render apps and they differ in viewport, theme, capabilities, and consent policy. Your app degrades, and your only choice is whether the degraded version is designed. Five rungs, and the fourth one, plain text, is the one most of your users get. Test two hosts rather than eleven, lie to your own app about capabilities to see what breaks, and generate your compatibility table so its staleness is visible.
