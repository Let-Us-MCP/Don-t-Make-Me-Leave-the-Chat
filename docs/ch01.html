Chapter 1

Don't Make Me Leave the Chat

The first law, the three questions that enforce it, and the same task built three ways.

Here is the first law, and if you only remember one thing from this book, make it this one:

Law 1

Don’t make me leave the chat.

That is most of the book. Everything else is footnotes, examples, and me talking you out of the dashboard you are already planning.

Your app is a guest

An MCP App does not open in a tab. It does not get a URL. Nobody typed your name into anything. Your app appears in the middle of a conversation that was going fine without you, because a model decided your tool was useful for the next thirty seconds of somebody’s task. That makes you a guest. A welcome one, hopefully, but a guest, and guests have exactly one job: help with the thing that is happening, then get out of the way. Guests do not rearrange the furniture, demand everyone’s attention, or announce that actually, the real party is at their house.

You would be amazed how many widgets announce that the real party is at their house.

The comparison to a web page is worth making precise, because it is where most of the bad instincts come from. A web page is a destination, and somebody navigated to it. They typed a URL, or clicked a link, or opened a bookmark, and in doing so they made a decision: for the next few minutes, this page has my attention. The page can spend that attention. It has a header, a nav bar, a footer, and a cookie banner, and none of those is outrageous, because the user signed up for a place and a place has furniture.

An MCP App is not a place. It is a paragraph that happens to have buttons. It arrives in a stream of other paragraphs, in a column a few hundred pixels wide, underneath a sentence somebody typed and above a sentence somebody is about to type. Nobody signed up for it, nobody can bookmark it, and the user did not choose you. The model did, on their behalf, about two seconds ago, and it will choose somebody else in a minute. Design for the paragraph, not for the place, and most of the rest of this book follows without being told.

Why leaving is so expensive

“Leave the chat” sounds dramatic, and most apps do not literally eject anyone. They do something quieter: they break the flow. In this medium the flow is worth more than it has ever been worth anywhere else, because the conversation is holding three things at once, and a break costs all three.

The first is the context. Everything established so far, which by the time your tool fires is usually quite a lot: the account being discussed, the date range that matters, the option already ruled out, the tone the user has settled into. None of that is written down anywhere the user can point at. It exists as a shared understanding between a person and a model, and the moment your app behaves as though none of it happened, the user has to carry all of it across the gap by hand, in a text box, one fact at a time.

The second is momentum. The user was mid-task and had the next step in their head. Thought is perishable in a way that is easy to underrate when you are the one being interesting: every second your widget spends being impressive instead of useful is a second that next step is evaporating, and when it has evaporated the user does not blame your load time. They lose interest in the task.

The third is new, and Chapter 2 is entirely about it. The model is tracking the task too, and it can only track what reaches it. Send the user off to a tab, a login page, or a maze of controls the model cannot see into, and you have not just interrupted a person. You have blinded the other participant, and the user will discover this three turns later when they ask a follow-up question and get an answer that ignores everything they just did. A web page that wastes your attention costs you attention. A chat app that breaks the flow costs the context, the momentum, and the model, and the user pays to rebuild every one of them.

So the bar is high, which brings us to the uncomfortable part.

An app must earn its render

Text is the incumbent, and the incumbent is good. Text streams instantly, token by token, so the user is reading the answer while it is still being written. Text is quotable, searchable, and screen-readable. The model can refer back to it, and so can the user, six turns later, by scrolling. Text needs no viewport, no theme, no consent prompt, no load time, and no host capability negotiation. It works in every client that has ever spoken this protocol and every client that ever will. Your widget is competing with all of that, and before you ship a render it has to win.

Three questions tell you whether it will. Ask them in order, and be honest, because your users will be.

Does this need to be seen, or just read? A trend needs to be seen. A number needs to be read. “Revenue is up 12% to $4.2M” is a finished answer; a chart that says the same thing is a slower paragraph with a loading state. Shape, comparison, spatial arrangement, a picture of the actual thing: seen. A value, a status, a verdict, a fact: read. The test is not whether a picture would be nice, because a picture is always nice. The test is whether the picture carries something the sentence cannot, and the way to run it is to try writing the caption. If the caption is the answer, ship the caption and keep the afternoon.

Will the user act on it, or just glance at it? If they are going to pick, adjust, approve, or explore, then interactivity is doing real work and the render is probably justified. If they are going to look once and move on, you have shipped controls to somebody who wanted a sentence, and controls are not free: they occupy the space where the answer would have gone. Glances want billboards, not cockpits, and Chapter 3 is entirely about designing for the glance. Watch for the tell in testing, because it is unmissable: a control nobody touches in five sessions is a control that failed this question, and the model-as-user runs in Chapter 11 measure the same thing without recruiting anybody.

Does the conversation already contain the answer? The cruellest failure in this medium is a widget that renders four input fields when the user already said, in words, in this very conversation, what goes in three of them. If the conversation contains it, your app should arrive already knowing it, and arrive showing that it knows. This one is important enough that it gets its own law in Chapter 4.

Score zero out of three and the right widget count is zero, so write the sentence and move on. Score one and think hard about whether the one you scored is really worth a render. Two or three, render, and make it count.

The questions, applied

The three questions look soft until you run them on things you have actually seen, so here they are against six tools that exist in some form on every MCP server being built right now.

The tool Seen? Act? New? Verdict
“What is our error rate right now” no no yes A sentence. Ship no widget.
“Show me the last 30 days of signups” yes no yes A chart, and nothing else on the card.
“Which of these three candidates should I schedule” yes yes partly A picker. This is the sweet spot.
“File this receipt” no yes no One confirmation, everything prefilled.
“What does the contract say about X” partly no yes Quoted text beats a viewer.
“Rebalance this budget” yes yes no A canvas. Direct manipulation earns it.

Two things fall out of that table. The middle column decides most cases: if the user is going to act, some interface usually beats some prose even when the data is unremarkable, and if they are only going to look, the bar is much higher and most widgets do not clear it. And row four is the one people get wrong most often, because “file this receipt” feels like a form when it is actually a confirmation with four facts attached. Chapter 4 is about the difference, and it is worth about eight fields.

Teardown: the same task, three ways

The task: somebody comparing three loan quotes asks, “which of these is actually cheapest over five years?”

The gallery builds this three ways. Run them yourself with node gallery/serve.js and click through the Chapter 1 scenes; the argument survives on paper, but it lands in the renderer, where you can drag the viewport down to 380 pixels and watch two of the three fall apart.

Exhibit A: prose

Total cost of ownership, one line per lender, cheapest marked, done. It streams in under a second, the model can cite it three turns later without another tool call, and for a one-shot answer this is the ceiling. It costs nothing and it never goes stale in a way anybody misreads, because a sentence in a transcript reads as a record of a moment, which is what it is.

Notice the last line of that answer, because it is the reason this chapter has two more exhibits. The ranking changes with the horizon. Northbank is cheapest for the first three years and Cedar Credit Union from year four on, because Cedar’s lower rate takes that long to pay off its $1,400 fee. That is a genuinely useful thing to know and it invites an immediate follow-up question, and follow-up questions cost turns: a tool call, a wait, and a new paragraph, every time the user wants to move the number.

Exhibit B: the kitchen sink

Somebody was proud of this one. It has tabs. It has an export button. It has a date range picker for a question that specified the range in the question. Count the annotations: five controls, zero of which the task asked for, the answer rendered as an unsorted table you have to compute yourself, and a settings gear, in a chat, which is the interface equivalent of a guest asking where you keep the fuse box.

The user asked which loan is cheapest. This widget answers, “well, what would you like to configure first?” It also fails question one without noticing: the data here does not need to be seen, it needs to be ranked, which is a computation rather than a picture, and the widget declined to do it. Every pixel of chrome in that figure is a decision to make the user do arithmetic in a column 380 pixels wide.

Exhibit C: the right-sized card

One card, three rows, and the verdict as the headline in the largest type on screen, before anything else resolves. And exactly one control: a horizon slider, because “over five years” was an assumption rather than a fact, and it is the kind of assumption a user actually wants to poke at. Drag it to four years and the winner changes in front of you. That slider is the entire reason Exhibit C beats Exhibit A, and it earns its place by passing question two: the user will act on this, repeatedly, in a way that would otherwise cost a conversational turn each time.

Notice what produced Exhibit C. Not restraint as a virtue, which is a disposition rather than a method, but the three questions used as a filter. Seen? Yes, a ranking that reorders. Act? Yes, one adjustable assumption. Already answered? Partly, and everything already answered is pre-filled and shown rather than asked. Notice also what Exhibit C did not drop: every number Exhibit B showed is still there, APR and monthly payment and fees, moved from a table into a subtitle under each row where they are available to anyone who cares and invisible to anyone who does not. Omitting needless widgets is not the same as omitting information, and Chapter 5 spends its length on the difference because the confusion between them produces both of the failure modes in this teardown.

What a good render costs

The three questions are a filter, and a filter is only worth using if the thing being filtered has a real cost. It does, and the cost is worth spelling out, because most teams price a render at zero and then wonder why the medium feels cluttered.

A render is a fetch, so the host has to have your template, and if it did not prefetch it the user waits. A render is a decision, because the user has to work out whether to engage with it, which is cheap once and expensive across a day. A render is a surface: it occupies the column, everything above it moves up and out of view, and on a phone one card can be the entire screen, which means the conversation the user was having is now off it. A render is a consent boundary, because the moment it has a button the user is in a policy conversation with their host about what your server may do. And a render is a thing that can be stale in a way a sentence cannot, because a live-looking card reads as the present tense whether or not it has checked anything recently, which is the whole of Chapter 7.

None of that is a reason never to render. All of it is a reason the answer has to be worth it, and together it explains why the incumbent is so much stronger here than it ever was on the web. On the web, the alternative to your interface was somebody else’s interface. Here the alternative is a sentence that arrives instantly, works everywhere, and never claims to know something it stopped checking four minutes ago.

The objections, handled

You have heard these already, or you will, probably in a planning meeting where the roadmap is already drawn.

“But engagement.” Somewhere, a dashboard is being pitched because time-in-widget will look excellent in the metrics. In a conversation, time-in-widget is not engagement; it is delay with a progress bar. The user’s goal has a finish line, and the flattering interpretation of them lingering in your UI is rarely the true one. Measure task completion and re-invocation instead, because an app that finishes fast gets summoned again tomorrow, and the thing doing the summoning is a model that has read your description and formed a view about what you are for. That is engagement, it compounds, and Chapter 13 is about how it compounds.

“But our whole product.” The temptation to port the SaaS into the iframe is enormous, because the SaaS already exists and somebody already paid for it. Resist it. Your product was designed for a full screen, a logged-in session, a navigation model, and a user who arrived on purpose, and not one of those four things is true here. The chat version of your product is not your product, smaller. It is the thirty seconds of your product that this task needs, and everything else you bring is the tour nobody asked for. Patterns of MCP App Architecture calls this failure mode the SPA-in-a-box and has architectural reasons to hate it. This book just has taste, and taste gets there faster.

“But the model cannot do that in text.” Correct, sometimes, and that is exactly what the three questions are for. When the answer is genuinely visual, genuinely interactive, or genuinely faster to manipulate than to describe, then render it with a clear conscience and put the work in. This book is not anti-app; it is anti needless app, and the difference between those two positions is a test rather than a vibe. Chapter 11 shows you how to run the test for the price of one afternoon and about ten cents.

“Our users like it.” Maybe. Five of them, watched for an hour, will tell you whether that is true, and the answer will not be ambiguous. It never is.

The one thing

A good MCP App is the guest who shows up with exactly the tool the moment needs, uses it, hands the result back into the conversation, and lets the flow continue as though they had always been part of it. Nobody remembers that guest as impressive. They remember the evening as having gone well, which is the only review an interface in somebody else’s conversation is ever going to get.

Don’t make me leave the chat. Not for a tab, not for a login, not for a tour of your tabs and settings. The conversation was the point, and you were invited to help with it.
